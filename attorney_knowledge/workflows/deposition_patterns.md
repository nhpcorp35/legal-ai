# Deposition Analysis

## What Attorneys Actually Ask

Across multiple depositions, attorneys repeatedly focus on:

### Identity

* Who are you?
* What is your role?
* What is your job title?
* Who do you work for?

### Knowledge

* What do you personally know?
* What did you personally observe?
* What did someone else tell you?
* What do you not remember?

### Responsibility

* Who made the decision?
* Who supervised the work?
* Who controlled the work?
* Who had authority?

### Timeline

* When did it happen?
* What happened first?
* What happened next?
* When did you become aware?

### Documents

* Have you seen this document?
* Did you sign this document?
* Did you read this document?
* Is this your signature?

### Communication

* Who did you speak with?
* What was discussed?
* Was anything put in writing?
* Were emails exchanged?

### Prior Statements

* Did you previously testify differently?
* Did you previously state something else?
* Did you sign a document saying something different?

---

## What Attorneys Are Trying To Prove

### Personal Knowledge

The witness actually knows the facts.

or

The witness is repeating what someone else told them.

### Responsibility

Who is legally responsible?

Examples:

* Owner
* Contractor
* Subcontractor
* Architect
* Engineer
* Witness

### Timeline

Whether events occurred in the sequence claimed.

### Control

Who had authority?

Who directed the work?

Who made decisions?

### Notice

Who knew about the issue?

When did they know?

### Credibility

Can the witness be trusted?

Has the witness changed their story?

### Damages

What harm occurred?

When?

Why?

---

## What Attorneys Are Trying To Eliminate

### Speculation

"I think."

"I assume."

"Someone told me."

Attorneys want facts, not guesses.

### Hearsay

The witness repeating what others said.

### Lack of Knowledge

The witness claiming knowledge they do not possess.

### Alternative Explanations

Any explanation that weakens their theory of the case.

### Shared Responsibility

Attempts to shift blame.

### Memory Excuses

"I don't remember."

"I don't recall."

Repeated memory failures may reduce usefulness.

---

## What Makes Testimony Useful

### First-Hand Knowledge

The witness personally saw it.

The witness personally heard it.

The witness personally did it.

### Specificity

Names.

Dates.

Documents.

Locations.

Precise events.

### Consistency

The testimony matches:

* Prior testimony
* Affidavits
* Documents
* Physical evidence

### Verification

The testimony can be confirmed through:

* Emails
* Contracts
* Photographs
* Reports
* Other witnesses

### Admissions

The witness admits:

* Responsibility
* Knowledge
* Control
* Notice
* Mistakes

Admissions are highly valuable.

---

## What Makes Testimony Dangerous

### Contradictions

The witness changes their story.

### Sworn Statement Conflicts

Affidavit says one thing.

Deposition says another.

### Knowledge Conflicts

Witness claims:

"I don't know."

but previously claimed knowledge.

### Timeline Conflicts

Events occur in different sequences.

Dates change.

### Document Conflicts

Testimony conflicts with:

* Contracts
* Emails
* Reports
* Photographs
* Court filings

### Impeachment Material

Anything showing:

* Inconsistency
* Falsehood
* Evasion
* Selective memory

### Absolute Statements

"Never."

"Always."

"No one."

"Nothing."

Absolute statements are dangerous because a single contrary fact may destroy credibility.

---

## Attorney Observation

The purpose of a deposition is not simply to learn facts.

The purpose is often to determine:

* What the witness actually knows
* What the witness can prove
* What the witness cannot prove
* Whether the witness is credible
* Whether the witness can be impeached
* Whether the testimony helps or hurts the case

A deposition is frequently a credibility and responsibility investigation.
